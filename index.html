Generic AI discovery framework

Agnostic questions, document requests, and red flags you can use with any new client — regardless of industry, size, or maturity. Adapt the framing; the underlying questions apply universally.
How to use this framework
Seven discovery tracks run in parallel across two or three sessions. Each track has a different primary owner — some questions go to the MD/CEO, others to operations heads, others to the people who do the work day-to-day. Never ask them all in one room.
The golden rule of AI discovery: You are not discovering whether AI can help — it almost always can. You are discovering whether this business is ready to absorb it, in what sequence it will create the most value, and what has to be true before the first recommendation can land. Readiness precedes strategy.
Session 1 — Leadership pair
Business fundamentals, strategic intent, go/no-go parameters, investment envelope. Run MD and GM separately first if the business has both roles. Combine for alignment check.
Session 2 — Operational heads
Process maturity, data and systems reality, people and change readiness. The people who run the processes know whether the documentation matches practice. Go to them, not the CEO, for current state truth.
Session 3 — Ground level
Shadow real work. One briefing, one job handoff, one client communication. Ten minutes of observation is worth three interviews. What you see in practice is the baseline your AI will operate in — not what's written down.

Track 2 — Process maturity
The most important discovery track for AI readiness. AI enforces and accelerates processes — it cannot create them from scratch. You need to know whether processes are documented, practised, and consistent.
Process documentation reality
Ops heads
Do you have documented processes for your core workflows — where do they live?
When were they last updated? Are they actively maintained or written once and forgotten?
If I asked three different people how a job gets handed from sales to delivery, would I get the same answer?
Where do jobs most commonly get stuck or slow down?
What are the top three things that cause rework, errors, or delays right now?
Are there informal workarounds that people use instead of the documented process?
Handoffs and accountability
Ops heads
When work moves from one team or person to another, how does that happen — email, call, system?
Who is accountable when something falls through the gap between two teams?
How do you know a handoff was completed? Is there any record?
Where are the most expensive handoff failures — what do they cost when they go wrong?
How is scope change or variation from the original agreement handled?
Documents to request
5 real examples of a core handoff document — accepted and rejected versions
Any process maps, SOPs, or internal playbooks
A sample client contract or service agreement
A sample completed job record — from start to invoice
Any current checklists used in the field or in office
Maturity scoring guide

Level 1 — Informal: Work happens but isn't documented. Processes exist in people's heads. High key-person risk.

Level 2 — Documented: Processes written down but inconsistently followed. Docs may be outdated.

Level 3 — Practised: Processes followed consistently. Some measurement. Deviations noticed.

Level 4 — Measured: KPIs tracked against process. Issues visible before they escalate.

Level 5 — Optimised: Process data drives improvement. Ready for AI automation layer.

Listen for
"We know what to do, people just don't always do it" — adoption problem, not AI problem
Different answers from different people about the same process
Handoffs that happen verbally — no paper trail means no AI leverage
The real process described as an exception to the documented one

Business-specific AI / LLM build framework

A structured methodology for designing, building, and deploying a custom AI layer for a specific business. Works for any company where discovery has been completed and readiness has been assessed. Eight layers — each depends on the one before it.
The eight-layer build model
A business-specific LLM is not a chatbot — it is a purpose-built intelligence layer that knows how the business works, where the rules are, who owns what, and what good output looks like. Each layer enables the one above it.
The core distinction: A generic LLM knows everything about the world but nothing about your business. A business-specific AI knows everything about your business — its processes, terminology, clients, data, and rules — and uses that knowledge to generate outputs that are immediately useful without translation. The gap between generic and specific is the gap between "interesting" and "valuable."

Layer 1 — Knowledge architecture
Before writing a single prompt, define what the AI needs to know. Business knowledge has four types — each requires a different approach to capture, structure, and maintain it.
1A
Static knowledge — how the business works
Operating model, processes, rules, org structure
What it includesProcess documentation, gate rules, checklists, silo boundaries, decision authority, terminology glossary, service catalogue, standard contract terms
How to capture itCollect all existing documentation. Where it doesn't exist, conduct structured interviews. Convert to a consistent format — markdown or structured JSON. This becomes the AI's operating manual.
How to maintain itAssign an owner for each domain. Version-controlled. Updated when process changes — not annually in a review. Stale static knowledge is worse than none.
1B
Dynamic knowledge — live business state
Current jobs, pipeline, clients, financial position
What it includesActive job records, client history, pipeline status, AR aging, outstanding variations, current forward schedule, staff certifications, subcontractor register
How to capture itAPI connections to live systems — CRM, job management, accounting platform. Where APIs don't exist, structured database sync on a defined cadence.
Freshness requirementDefine acceptable staleness per data type. Job status: real-time. AR aging: daily. Revenue actuals: monthly. Stale dynamic data produces confident wrong answers.
1C
Historical knowledge — patterns and precedent
Completed jobs, past decisions, historical performance
What it includesCompleted job records with actuals vs quoted, prior SoWs and Gate packs, historical financial data, past client correspondence, variation history, incident reports
How to capture itStructured export and ingestion of historical system data. Document parsing for unstructured historical files. Define how far back — typically 24–36 months minimum for pattern recognition to be meaningful.
Primary usePattern detection — what jobs overrun, what clients dispute, what scopes are typically incomplete. Informs AI recommendations for future similar situations.
1D
Institutional knowledge — expertise & judgement
The stuff in people's heads that isn't written down
What it includesPricing intuition, client relationship nuance, site-specific knowledge, subcontractor assessments, escalation judgement, safety experience
How to capture itStructured interviews with key people. Scenario-based questioning: "what do you do when X?" Convert to decision trees or rule sets. This is the hardest knowledge to capture and the most valuable.
Critical noteThis knowledge often lives in one or two people. Its capture is also a key-person risk mitigation exercise — not just an AI input.

Layer 3 — Model selection and architecture
Three architectural approaches — each with different cost, control, and capability profiles. Most business-specific AI systems use RAG as the primary approach, with prompt engineering for structure and fine-tuning reserved for specific, high-volume tasks.
Approach 1
Prompt engineering
Start here

Structured prompts with business context injected at runtime. The model receives the operating rules, the specific task, and relevant data — and produces the output.

Best for: Document generation, completeness checking, classification, drafting. Any task where the rules are definable and the output is structured.

Not suitable for: Tasks requiring deep reasoning over large document sets, or where context exceeds prompt window limits.

Low costFast to deployEasy to update
Primary approach
Approach 2
RAG — retrieval-augmented generation

Business knowledge stored in a vector database. At query time, the most relevant knowledge chunks are retrieved and injected into the prompt context. The model answers using retrieved business knowledge.

Best for: Large knowledge bases (documentation, job history, client records), question answering, document search, context-aware generation across many sources.

Key requirement: Clean, structured source documents. Garbage in, garbage out — RAG amplifies data quality issues.

Knowledge stays currentNo retrainingTraceable sources
Approach 3
Fine-tuning
Use sparingly

The base model is retrained on business-specific examples — teaching it the specific output format, tone, and patterns of this business. Expensive and requires retraining when processes change.

Best for: Very high-volume, consistent tasks where format is critical and examples number in the thousands — e.g. auto-coding transactions, classifying job types.

Not suitable for: Most business AI use cases. Prompt engineering + RAG achieves equivalent results with less cost and more flexibility.

High costRetraining requiredUse for specific tasks
Recommended model stack for a business-specific AI

Base model: Claude (Anthropic) or GPT-4o (OpenAI) for primary generation tasks. Both have long context windows, strong instruction following, and JSON output modes. Claude has stronger document analysis; GPT-4o has broader tool ecosystem.

Embedding model: OpenAI text-embedding-3-large or Cohere embed-v3 for RAG vector search. Creates the semantic search layer over your business knowledge base.

Vector database: Pinecone (managed, fast), Weaviate (open source, flexible), or pgvector (Postgres extension — lowest friction if you're already on Postgres).

Orchestration: LangChain or LlamaIndex for connecting retrieval, prompt construction, and generation. Vercel AI SDK if the team is Node.js-native (aligns with FM Farm stack).

Layer 4 — Interface design per user type
Different users need different interfaces to the same underlying AI. A CEO dashboard, a field crew mobile form, and a finance admin document review are all driven by the same AI layer — but they look and behave completely differently.
Interface types by user

Leadership (MD / GM): Dashboard with KPI cards, exception register, and trend charts. AI surfaces anomalies and assigns owners. No prompting required — the intelligence is pushed, not pulled.

Silo heads (Commercial / Ops / Finance): Workflow-embedded AI — appears at specific steps in an existing process. Gate pack review, quote generation assist, financial anomaly flag. Integrated into the tool they already use.

State managers: Document generation and review interface. Input job parameters, receive a pre-populated document. Review, edit, submit. AI is invisible — they see a better form, not an AI.

Field crew (supervisors): Mobile-first, minimal interface. Structured photo upload, daily timesheet, variation flag form. Maximum 3 taps to complete any action. Offline-first. No chat interface — guided forms only.

Finance administrator: Document review interface with AI pre-population. Gate 2 checklist pre-checked from submitted pack. Invoice draft pre-generated. Exception flags with suggested actions.

Interface design principles

AI should be invisible at field level. A supervisor on site who's been doing this job for 10 years will not use a chatbot. The AI surface for field crews is a structured form that submits to the right place automatically — they don't know or care that AI processed it.

Leadership sees outputs, not inputs. The MD doesn't want to prompt an AI. They want a screen that tells them the three things that need their attention today. Push intelligence to them.

Middle management reviews, not generates. State managers and silo heads are reviewers and editors, not authors. AI drafts, they approve. This is faster and they retain control.

Chat is a power user interface, not a default. A freeform chat interface is useful for silo heads asking complex questions. It is not the right interface for most business AI interactions.

Questions to ask about interface
What does the user do 10–20 times a day that this AI should help with?
What device do they use for that task — and when, and where?
What is the minimum viable interaction — can it be under 30 seconds?
What do they do today that the AI should replace — not add to their workload?
Layer 5 — Governance, guardrails, and rules
A business-specific AI must know not just what it can do, but what it must not do. Governance defines the boundaries, the human-in-the-loop requirements, and the audit trail. This is designed before the first line of code, not added after.
Autonomy levels — what AI can do alone vs with approval

Fully autonomous (no human required): Completeness checking, SLA timers, notification sending, data retrieval, document pre-population, read-only reporting.

AI drafts, human approves: Document generation (SoW, Gate packs, invoices, reports), exception owner assignment, escalation routing, client communications.

AI informs, human decides: Variation pricing, contract terms, hiring decisions, strategic recommendations, anything with commercial or legal consequence.

AI never touches: Final financial authorisations, client relationship decisions, performance management, safety stop-work authority, anything requiring a licensed professional judgement.

Non-negotiable guardrails

Confidence thresholds: The AI must express uncertainty when it is uncertain. "I don't have enough information" is a valid and required output. False confidence is worse than no answer.

Source traceability: Every AI output that draws on specific knowledge must cite the source. Reviewers need to know where a claim came from to trust and verify it.

Audit trail: All AI-generated content that enters a business process must be logged — what was generated, when, by whom, what prompt was used, what the user did with it.

Override always available: Any AI recommendation or output must be overridable by a human without friction. The AI assists; the human decides.

Data privacy: Client names, financial figures, and personal data never leave the business's controlled environment. No sending sensitive data to external AI APIs without proper data handling agreements in place.

Layer 7 — Rollout and adoption design
The rollout is not a training event — it is a sustained behaviour change programme. Design it that way from the start. The pilot selection and champion identification matter as much as the technology.
Pilot design principles

Pick the highest-readiness state or team, not the hardest problem. The first pilot should produce a visible win fast. Success breeds adoption. Failure in a pilot kills momentum across the whole business.

One use case, fully embedded, before the second. A partial rollout of five use cases produces five half-adopted tools. One use case that becomes habitual produces the platform for everything else.

Champion first, mandate second. Identify the person who will love this — naturally curious, trusted by peers, early adopter. Make them the champion before any broader rollout. Their credibility carries adoption where top-down instruction doesn't.

Measure during the pilot, not after. Define what success looks like before launch. Instrument usage, output quality, and time saved from day one. Data from the pilot justifies the rollout.

The 90-day adoption sequence

Days 1–14 — Champion onboarding: Champion uses the tool on real work. Daily feedback loop. Fixes and refinements made in real time. Champion builds the internal narrative.

Days 15–30 — Small group pilot: 3–5 users from the same team. Champion leads peer training. No formal training sessions — learning happens on real jobs.

Days 31–60 — Measure and refine: Quantify time saved, error rate reduction, output quality. Fix the top 3 friction points identified in the pilot. Build the business case for broader rollout.

Days 61–90 — Broader rollout: Champion presents pilot results to the wider team. Top-down endorsement from the GM. New users onboarded with champion support, not IT support. Formal training is a one-pager and a 20-minute demo, not a course.

Layer 8 — System prompt architecture and templates
The system prompt is the operating constitution of a business-specific AI. It tells the model who it is, what it knows, what it must and must not do, and how to format its outputs. This is written once and maintained as the business evolves.
The system prompt structure: Every business AI system prompt has the same five sections — identity, knowledge context, task rules, output format, and guardrails. The sections below are templates. ## What you must always do - If you are uncertain, say so explicitly. Do not generate a confident answer when the information is insufficient. - Cite the specific source or rule when making a claim that comes from the business knowledge base. - Flag if an input conflicts with a known operating rule — do not resolve the conflict yourself. - Produce outputs in the format specified. Structure and consistency matter more than completeness. ## What you must never do - Do not make pricing or commercial decisions — flag for human review. - Do not communicate directly to clients — generate drafts for human approval. - Do not approve safety-critical decisions — the WHS Manager / safety function has final authority. - Do not generate outputs that could be mistaken for a final decision when human approval is required. - Do not store, repeat, or reference sensitive personal data beyond what is required for the immediate task. ## If you cannot complete the task State what information is missing and which person or system would have it. Do not attempt to complete the task with insufficient information.

Measuring success — the metrics that matter
Before you build anything, define what you will measure and how. The measurement framework is agreed with the client during scoping — not designed after deployment when success is contested.
Process efficiency metrics

Gate 1 first-pass acceptance rate (before / after). Gate 2 submission time from completion. Invoice cycle time Gate 2 → issued. Time to produce Gate 3 pack. Variation documentation rate.

Financial impact metrics

Days Sales Outstanding trend. Cashflow gap reduction (D0 to invoice). AR over 60 days as % of total. Invoice dispute rate. Gross margin actual vs quoted by job type.

Adoption and quality metrics

Daily active users per user type. AI output acceptance rate (used vs discarded). Time saved per task (surveyed). Errors caught by AI before passing downstream. User satisfaction score at 30 / 90 days.
